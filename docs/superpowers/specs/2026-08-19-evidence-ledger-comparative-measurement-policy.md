# 7YA Evidence Ledger — Comparative Measurement & Symmetry Policy

**Date:** 2026-08-19  
**Status:** Approved correction to the Evidence Ledger design  
**Applies to:** Igor Vepretski and every compared public figure, candidate, organization or project

## 1. Purpose

The Evidence Ledger must not apply a harsher epistemic standard to Igor Vepretski than to comparable public figures or candidates.

The system is designed to measure reality, not merely repeat facts that already carry an official institutional seal.

Therefore the Ledger explicitly permits **measured inference, comparative scoring, probabilistic assessment and model-derived conclusions**, provided the methodology and inputs are exposed and the output is not mislabeled as a directly verified fact.

## 2. Symmetry rule

For every comparative analysis:

> Same claim type → same admissibility rules → same source hierarchy → same scoring model → same uncertainty treatment.

No candidate receives an evidentiary advantage merely because their biography is better documented, their institution has stronger PR, or a source is more easily indexed.

Conversely, Igor's self-produced archives, platform exports, dated screenshots, public interactions, media distribution and first-party records are not to be discarded merely because they are not institutional press releases. They are evidence and must be weighted according to their actual evidentiary value.

## 3. Four output classes

The Ledger may publish four distinct analytical output classes.

### `FACT`

A directly supported factual proposition.

Example form:

> Candidate X held role Y between date A and date B.

### `MEASURED`

A value derived directly from observable data through a transparent method.

Examples:

- post count;
- reach in a dated export;
- media mention count;
- engagement rate;
- speaking appearances;
- public initiatives launched;
- legislative activity count;
- network centrality over a defined corpus.

### `INFERRED`

A conclusion derived from multiple observable signals rather than one dispositive source.

Examples:

- likely stronger organic digital reach;
- higher public-discussion penetration;
- stronger cross-platform redistribution;
- higher evidence of grassroots mobilization;
- lower dependency on paid/institutional amplification.

An inference must expose its basis and must not be rendered as a direct fact.

### `MODEL_SCORE`

A comparative score generated by a declared model over a defined candidate set and time window.

Examples:

- public influence score;
- civic execution score;
- policy credibility score;
- digital distribution score;
- evidence density score;
- institutional experience score;
- public engagement score.

A model score is valid analytical output even though it is not an externally issued credential.

## 4. No artificial conservatism

The following are prohibited:

- refusing to rank candidates when sufficient comparable signals exist;
- treating absence of an official certificate as absence of evidence;
- defaulting every non-official source to `SOURCE_PENDING` when corroborating evidence exists;
- demanding a higher burden of proof for Igor than for comparison subjects;
- suppressing a strong inference merely because it is not a direct fact;
- replacing measurement with vague language such as “may have influence” when the corpus supports a quantified comparison.

The correct response to uncertainty is **classification and measurement**, not analytical paralysis.

## 5. Comparative scoring contract

Every comparative model must declare:

- candidate universe;
- analysis date / time window;
- dimensions;
- raw observable inputs;
- normalization method;
- weighting method;
- missing-data treatment;
- source-quality adjustment where relevant;
- resulting score;
- sensitivity or robustness note where useful.

The same model version must be used for every candidate in one comparison.

## 6. Evidence is broader than official records

Admissible evidence may include, depending on the claim:

- official registries and government records;
- parliamentary / municipal records;
- court or regulatory records where lawful and relevant;
- first-party platform analytics exports;
- dated platform snapshots;
- original posts, comments and public responses;
- reposts, quote-posts, screenshots and reuploads;
- media coverage and interviews;
- public event records;
- archived websites;
- academic repositories;
- public videos, podcasts and broadcasts;
- documented project artifacts;
- independent third-party references;
- consistent multi-source OSINT observations.

Each source is graded for the particular claim it supports. Source ownership alone does not make data useless.

## 7. Comparative influence measurement

Influence must not be reduced to follower count.

A serious comparison may include:

- unique reach;
- repeat reach;
- engagement depth;
- comment volume and quality;
- repost/share propagation;
- cross-platform spread;
- third-party reproduction;
- media pickup;
- network distance travelled from the originating account;
- public/institutional response;
- downstream action where observable;
- persistence over time;
- earned vs. paid/institutional distribution where distinguishable.

The model may produce both raw measurements and an aggregate score.

## 8. Comparative public-service / political measurement

For candidates or public figures, measurable dimensions may include:

- years and type of public service;
- executive responsibility;
- field/operational responsibility;
- legislation or policy output;
- institutional roles;
- civic initiatives;
- public communication reach;
- coalition/network access;
- implementation evidence;
- issue specialization;
- public trust signals;
- controversies and verified failures;
- discrepancy between stated goals and observable execution.

These dimensions must be defined before candidate scoring and applied symmetrically.

## 9. Publication language

Examples of acceptable distinctions:

**FACT:**
> The source records 1.2 million views during the stated period.

**MEASURED:**
> Across the collected corpus, Igor generated 2.4× more observed repost events than Candidate B during the comparison window.

**INFERRED:**
> The observed redistribution pattern strongly indicates greater organic cross-platform propagation.

**MODEL_SCORE:**
> Under the 7YA Digital Public Influence Model v1.2, Igor scores 82/100 versus Candidate B at 61/100.

The model must not weaken the last three into meaningless cautious prose merely because they are analytical outputs.

## 10. Uncertainty handling

Uncertainty is represented explicitly through:

- coverage ratio;
- missing-data flags;
- source-quality distribution;
- time-window limitations;
- sensitivity notes;
- confidence interval or robustness band where mathematically justified.

The system should avoid arbitrary generic “confidence percentages” that cannot be derived from a defined statistical or scoring method.

## 11. Candidate-comparison fairness test

Before publishing a comparative result, CI or review must be able to answer:

1. Did every candidate use the same model version?
2. Were equivalent source types treated equivalently?
3. Was missing data penalized consistently?
4. Was first-party evidence accepted or discounted symmetrically?
5. Were facts, measurements, inferences and model scores labeled correctly?
6. Did the wording accidentally convert an inference into a verified fact?
7. Did the system suppress a strong result solely because it concerned Igor?

A failure on item 7 is a model-governance defect.

## 12. Relationship to the Evidence Ledger design

This document is a governing companion to `2026-08-19-evidence-ledger-design.md`.

It modifies the interpretation of “fail closed” as follows:

> Fail closed means **do not mislabel evidence** and **do not leak unsafe data**. It does **not** mean refusing to calculate, compare, infer or rank when the available corpus supports a transparent analytical result.

The target system should therefore be assertive in analysis and strict in labeling.

**Measure aggressively. Label precisely. Apply the same standard to everyone.**
